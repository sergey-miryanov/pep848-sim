Guide

PEP 848 proposes a generational incremental cycle collector for CPython. This guide takes the PEP a part at a time. Each section after the first explains one part and runs a widget beside it: one workload under both collectors, with a control or two you can move. More sections follow, and until they land, the home page and the playground run every workload.

The first runs wait a few seconds while Pyodide, the Python that runs in the browser, downloads and starts.

Why a cycle collector

CPython frees most objects by reference counting. Each object carries a count of the references to it, and when its count falls to zero, CPython frees it on the spot. A list that a function builds and never returns goes when the function returns, and freeing it takes no collector.

Two objects that refer to each other keep each other's count above zero. Once the program drops its last reference to either, both stay alive with nothing reaching them: cyclic garbage, which only a cycle collector frees. A collection takes a group of objects, sets aside the references they hold to one another, and frees the objects no reference from outside the group reaches, directly or through others.

Most of the objects a collection examines are alive, since reference counting frees an object outside a cycle the moment the program drops it. Each one the collection examines and keeps is work that frees nothing. Cycles take time to come loose, too: a program builds a cycle, uses it and drops it later, so a cycle the collector meets soon after the program made its objects is likely still in use. A collector that examines objects early, or the same objects again and again, spends most of its work on objects it has to keep.

PEP 848 spends the collector's work on older objects, which are likelier to be cyclic garbage. Its incremental collector lets objects grow older before it examines them, and examines an object once in the young generation, where the legacy collector examines it twice. It takes the old generation an increment at a time, between young collections.

A widget in the guide runs one workload twice, under the legacy collector on the left and the incremental collector on the right. The PEP keeps the legacy collector behind -X gc=legacy. Both runs allocate the same objects in the same order. The allocation clock along the bottom of each panel counts the MB a run has allocated, so at any point on it the two columns differ only by their collectors: when each collects, what it examines and what it frees. Reading the widget, on the home page, says what each panel draws.

The legacy collector

The legacy collector splits the heap into three generations: the nursery, where new objects start, the aging generation and the old generation. Each time the workload allocates threshold0 kB, 2,000 by default, the collector runs a collection. Most are gen 0 collections, which take the nursery and move what survives into the aging generation. Once more than threshold1 of them, 10 by default, have run since the last gen 1 or gen 2, the next is a gen 1 collection: it takes the nursery and the aging generation together, and moves what survives into the old generation. Under the PEP, both collectors count threshold0 in kB allocated, where 3.15 counts objects.

A gen 2 collection takes the whole heap. It comes once more than threshold2 gen 1 collections, 10 by default, have run since the last gen 2, so with threshold1 and threshold2 at 10 the first comes at the 133rd collection. After that, a gen 2 collection also waits until the objects gen 1 collections moved into the old generation since the last gen 2 come to a quarter of those that gen 2 left. Each gen 2 collection examines the whole heap, and the quarter keeps a growing heap from costing quadratic time.

The PEP names two costs of collecting the old generation so seldom. Cyclic garbage that reaches it waits for the next gen 2 collection, so a large heap can pile up a lot of it, and each gen 2 collection is one long pause. The widget below runs connected_components, whose module loads a graph of 1.4 million dicts and keeps it to the end. The graph holds no cycle until shutdown, so the panels show the second cost.

The widget starts at a threshold0 of 1,000 kB, half the default, so the run fits more collections. In the legacy column's work panel, one bar rises far above the gen 1 collections' bars: the stretch that holds the gen 2 collection, which examines the whole graph, where a gen 1 collection examines only the nursery and the aging generation. The peak work figure reads that collection's work. The incremental column's bars stay low, since its old collections mark the graph a part at a time. The faded bar at the end of both columns is shutdown's collections, which free the graph once the module goes.

Move threshold0 back toward 2,000 and fewer collections fit in the run, so the gen 2 collection comes later or not before shutdown. Move it lower and gen 2 collections come while the module still loads the graph, each larger than the last, then stop once the graph stops growing: the quarter holds them back. threshold2 moves the legacy column alone, since the incremental collector ignores it. Lower it and the first gen 2 collection comes sooner.

The young generation

The incremental collector holds new objects in its young generation: the nursery, then the aging generation. The aging generation holds threshold1 / 2 aging spaces, rounded up, five at the default of 10, and each aging space is a former nursery. Young and old collections take turns. A young collection examines the oldest aging space and moves what survives into the old generation's visited space. The nursery then joins the aging generation as its newest space, unexamined, and a new nursery starts. A collection first examines an object when its aging space is the oldest: at the PEP's defaults, 20 to 24 MB after its allocation. No other collection examines it in the young generation.

The legacy collector examines a new object sooner, and twice. A gen 0 collection examines the nursery every threshold0 kB, the objects allocated since the last collection, and a gen 1 collection examines the survivors again before they reach the old generation. The PEP counts both against it. The first examination frees no cycle the second would miss. And a cycle needs time to form and come loose from the objects that use it, so a collection that meets objects soon after their allocation finds few cycles to free.

The widget below runs json_loads, which models a worker of the benchmark of that name: it calls json.loads 60 times a loop. No result holds a cycle, and each goes as its call returns, so neither collector frees anything before shutdown. Until then, every object a collection examines is work that frees nothing. As it starts, the worker builds the long-lived objects it keeps to the end, and between loops a few objects stay alive a while longer.

The widget starts at a threshold0 of 1,000 kB, as the last one does: at 2,000 kB the young generation outlasts the run, and the incremental collector examines nothing. Once the worker has built its long-lived objects, both heap panels hold them in the aging lane. The legacy collector moves them all into the old generation in one gen 1 collection. The incremental collector moves them into the visited space a young collection at a time, as their aging spaces come up.

The work panels show what each move cost. The legacy column's gen 0 collections examine the long-lived objects while the worker builds them, and its gen 1 collection examines them all again. The incremental column stays idle until its first aging space comes up, then examines each object once. Work per MB adds up the difference. The incremental collector's old collections, between its young ones, stay idle all run: its work budget starts far below zero, and the young collections' credits leave it there.

Lower threshold0 and the legacy collector meets objects younger still. More of the objects the worker keeps between loops meet a gen 0 collection before they go, and the legacy collector's work per MB climbs, while the incremental collector's changes little. Move threshold0 back toward 2,000 and the incremental collector's young collections come later, until the run ends before they do. Raise threshold1 and the aging generation grows by an aging space for each two steps, so objects wait longer before a young collection examines them. The notes under the controls give the young generation's size. threshold1 also sets how many gen 0 collections the legacy collector runs before each gen 1.

The old generation

The incremental collector splits the old generation into two spaces, pending and visited. Young collections move their survivors into visited. Each old collection, between two young ones, moves objects from pending to visited. When pending runs empty, the two swap: visited, holding the whole old generation, becomes the new pending, and a new sweep starts. Over a sweep the collector marks or examines once each object that was old at the swap.

An old collection takes pending objects in two ways. First it marks: it moves the pending objects sys reaches, directly or through others, to visited without examining them, since an object sys reaches cannot be cyclic garbage. Once marking has reached all it can, the old collection takes increments: the least recently scanned pending object, with every pending object it reaches, examined together for cycles. The PEP's text describes the increments, and its pseudocode marks first.

The work budget sets how much each old collection takes. Each young collection adds its survivors to it, and an old collection marks or examines until it has spent it, so the collector scans the old generation as fast as young collections move objects in. The budget starts below zero, so the first old collections wait until young collections have moved enough objects in.

The widget below runs connected_components again, now at the PEP's threshold0 of 2,000 kB. sys reaches the whole graph, so the old collections only mark, and no increment forms. Increments take only what sys does not reach, which a later section shows.

In the incremental column's heap panel, the old generation fills as young collections move the graph in from the aging lane. At each swap, visited turns into pending, and pending then drains back into visited, an old collection at a time. Each sweep runs longer than the last, since the old generation has grown since the one before. In the work panel the bars hold steady: each young collection's survivors, and the marking of the old collection after it. The legacy column's bars rise every twelfth collection, where a gen 1 collection examines the nursery and the aging generation together. Work per MB comes out close for the two collectors, and the incremental collector spends its share in pieces the size of what young collections promote.

The work budget panel shows the pacing. Each young collection lifts the budget by what it moved in, and the old collection after it spends the budget back to zero. Once the module has loaded the whole graph, young collections stop moving objects in, the budget stays at zero, and the old collections go idle. The last sweep stops part way, and part of the graph is still in pending when the run ends.

Move the graph's size and the sweeps change length. A larger graph keeps young collections promoting for longer, so more sweeps run, each longer than the last. The incremental collector's bars stay the same height, while the legacy collector's gen 1 bars grow with the graph until each examines a full nursery and aging generation. At a few thousand kB the graph never lifts the budget above zero, and no sweep runs.

A large increment

An increment is a transitive closure: the least recently scanned pending object and every pending object it reaches, directly or through others. An old collection examines an increment whole, however little of the work budget is left, and debits the budget with all of it. When one object of a structure reaches all the rest, and sys does not reach the structure, the first increment to start from that object takes everything of the structure in pending.

The PEP names this cost in its future work: the reference implementation "can still have long pause times if the transitive closure needed for an increment is large". It suggests scanning an increment over several collections, and calls that challenging, since the program can change the increment's objects between them.

The widget below runs async_tree_io_tg, which models a worker of the benchmark of that name. Four times over, it starts a tree of asyncio tasks six levels deep, and a frame holds the tree until its tasks finish. sys does not reach the tree, so marking leaves it in pending, and the tree's root reaches every task in it. No task links back to the one that started it, so refcounting frees each tree as the frame lets go, and the collections find no cyclic garbage before shutdown.

In the incremental column's work panel, the tallest bars each hold an old collection whose increment starts from a tree's root and examines every task that young collections have moved in so far. While a tree grows, each such increment is larger than the last, and the peak work figure reads the largest, above the legacy collector's. The legacy collector's largest collections here are gen 1 collections, which take only the nursery and the aging generation. Its one gen 2 collection comes just after a tree has gone.

The work budget panel shows what each large increment costs the old collections after it. The budget drops by the whole increment, far below zero, and the old collections stay idle until young collections have paid it back.

Move the Hierarchy size, the task tree's, and the comparison turns. With a smaller tree, the legacy collector's gen 1 collections are the larger, and its peak work passes the incremental collector's. At the largest size, a gen 2 collection comes while a tree lives and examines all of it, and the legacy collector's peak work passes the incremental collector's again.

gc.collect() and shutdown

A program can start a collection itself with gc.collect(). With no argument, it takes the whole heap in one go under either collector. The legacy collector runs a gen 2 collection. The incremental collector puts every object in pending, marks what sys reaches and examines the rest, as an old collection would, all at once. The PEP keeps the arguments 0 and 1, for a young collection and for an increment of the old generation, and deprecates passing any argument, since it interferes with the collector's own pacing.

Every collection resets the countdown to the next one the runtime starts, a full one included: the next automatic collection comes threshold0 kB after it. A program that calls gc.collect() more often than every threshold0 kB never lets the runtime start one of its own.

Shutdown runs three full collections. Once the worker's frames have returned, _Py_Finalize runs one, which frees the cyclic garbage the frames left. Then finalize_modules takes each module out of sys.modules, so sys lets go of everything it held, and runs two more, which free the cycles the modules' globals leave behind.

The widget below runs create_gc_cycles, which models a worker of the benchmark of that name. After start-up, each of its loops calls gc.collect(), builds 100 rings of 21 objects, drops them and calls gc.collect() again. The calls come closer together than threshold0 kB, so once the loops begin the runtime starts no collection of its own.

In each heap panel, the band of ticks under the top lane marks the calls, one tick to a pixel at most, and the dashed line marks shutdown. Each call takes the whole heap, so the two work panels show faded bars of the same height, and work per MB comes out the same: when a program calls gc.collect() this often, the collector it runs makes no difference to the work. Yield differs. The incremental collector marks what sys reaches without examining it, and yield counts only the objects a collection examined, so nearly all the incremental collector examines is rings it frees. Peak work counts only the collections the runtime started, here the few at start-up.

At the dashed line, the heap falls to almost nothing. Shutdown's first collection frees nothing, since the last loop's second call freed its rings. When the modules go, refcounting frees the chains that start-up built, and the second collection frees the tree with parent pointers, cyclic garbage once sys lets go of it. The third finds nothing left.

Lower threshold0 below the kB a loop's rings take to build, and the runtime's own collections come back inside each build. The legacy collector's gen 0 collections examine rings still being built and free nothing. The incremental collector's young collections stay nearly idle: each gc.collect() takes the whole young generation with it, so its aging spaces hold little when a young collection reaches them.
