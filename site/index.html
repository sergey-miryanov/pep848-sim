PEP 848: the incremental cycle collector

PEP 848 proposes a generational incremental cycle collector for CPython. This page runs a small Python simulator of it, and of the legacy collector beside it, in your browser: the widget below runs the workload you pick under both collectors and draws what each does. A guide built on such widgets comes later.

The first runs wait a few seconds while Pyodide, the Python that runs in the browser, downloads and starts.

Reading the widget

Both runs allocate the same objects in the same order: the legacy collector on the left, the incremental collector on the right. Each simulated object stands for 1 kB of real objects, and the allocation clock along the bottom counts the MB a run has allocated.

Rest the pointer on a panel to read its values: the heap before and after the nearest collection, the work budget that collection left, or the work in the bar under the pointer. Escape hides them until you move on.

Workload
Pick the workload both runs share. Each keeps the values you gave its controls while the page stays open, and the page's address names the one shown, so a link opens it.
Controls
Move a slider to pick a value, and release it to run both collectors again with it.
Figures
Each run's peak work, the most work one collection the runtime started did, which stands in for the worst pause; its peak uncollected garbage; its work per MB, which stands in for the collector's overhead; its yield, the share of the objects it examined that were cyclic garbage; its collections; and its gen 2 collections or sweeps.
Heap by space
The heap in MB, in lanes on the allocation clock. The top lane draws the whole heap and its reachable part, and shades the gap between them, the uncollected garbage. The lanes below draw the nursery, the aging generation and the old generation. In the incremental collector's column, the old generation's lane stacks its pending and visited spaces. A tick at the foot of the top lane marks each gc.collect() call, and a dashed rule across the panels marks shutdown.
Work
The objects each run's collections marked, saw survive and freed, summed over stretches of a few MB. The work of full collections, which gc.collect() and shutdown run, stacks faded above the runtime's own.
Work budget
The scanning the incremental collector owes, work_to_do in the PEP. Young collections add to it and old collections spend it; at zero or below, old collections do no work until young ones raise it again.

The workloads

The stand-in has 8 MB of module globals, a 20 MB tree rebuilt every 80 MB, and churn with 3% of its allocations in cycles. Presets will replace it, each modelled on a pyperformance benchmark and checked against that benchmark's yield. The guide comes after them.

create_gc_cycles, the first preset, models one worker of the pyperformance benchmark of that name. After start-up, each of its 512 loops calls gc.collect(), builds 100 rings of 21 objects, drops them and calls gc.collect() again, and shutdown ends the run. The calls reset the countdown to the next collection so often that the runtime starts none of its own once the loops begin. Until the benchmark runs again under gcmon, the start-up heap, the churn and the number of loops are placeholders.
